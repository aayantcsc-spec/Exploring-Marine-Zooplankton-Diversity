Eighty years of nets on the Mumbai coast

Zooplankton has been sampled off Mumbai since the 1940s, but never with the same net twice. What the record shows is partly the sea and partly the gear.

The record is thin and clustered

Nine sampling efforts across roughly eighty years, concentrated in two bursts: an estuarine-survey era around Thane Creek and the Ulhas, and a recent wave of short student and institute studies. Long stretches have no coverage at all.

1What this review argues

Zooplankton records for the Mumbai coast span roughly eighty years and several habitat types, yet they have never been read together. Doing so produces three findings. Copepods dominate every survey regardless of decade, habitat or method. Nutrient loading in the macro-tidal north appears to be restructuring the community away from crustacean plankton. And the apparent diversity of any given site is governed as much by net, depth and time of day as by the water itself — to the point that one recent beach survey recorded no meroplankton at all in a coastline where larval forms are routinely abundant. The practical conclusion is that Mumbai has no comparable time series, only a sequence of incompatible snapshots.

2What the record actually contains

The earliest material is indirect. Gaonkar, Krishnamurthy and Anil sampled fourteen stations in and around the ports of Mumbai across three periods in 2001 and 2002, and compared their results against records available since the 1940s — which means a mid-century baseline exists, held inside a later paper rather than as a standing dataset.

Between that baseline and the port survey, coverage is sparse and habitat-specific. A 1983 study of nearshore waters at Thal reported zooplankton biomass and composition, noting that diversity increased toward offshore stations and recording pycnogonids for the first time from the west coast of India. Nair and Ramaiah described the coastal ecosystem off Bombay in 1986.

The estuarine record is the densest, because Thane Creek and the Ulhas estuary have been resurveyed as pollution monitoring rather than as biodiversity work. A comparison against Gokhale and Athalye's 1993 figures found phytoplankton types had fallen from thirty-six to twenty-six, while the eleven zooplankton types recorded were comparable to 1993 but occurred at lower density. That is one of the few genuine before-and-after signals in the whole corpus, and it points at density rather than richness.

Recent work clusters again. Mangrove stations at Bhayandar, Patalganga and Lokhandwala yielded eleven zooplankton groups with tintinnids most dominant. A Patalganga estuary study identified fourteen groups, dominated by copepods and copepod nauplii, followed by barnacle nauplii and foraminifera. The Karanja estuary was sampled from January 2022 to March 2023, producing thirty-seven taxa from sixty samples, with copepods contributing 33.6% of total abundance and decapod larvae 16.75%. And the beach survey that prompted this review sampled five sites between September 2022 and February 2023, recording twenty-eight species in six orders and seventeen families.

3The one stable signal

Copepods dominate everywhere. They lead the Patalganga estuary counts, they contribute the single largest share at Karanja, they are the dominant group in the mangrove stations, and they are one of the two dominant groups on the open beaches alongside foraminifera. This holds across turbid estuary and sandy shore, across plankton nets and hand nets, and across four decades.

That consistency is worth stating plainly because it is the only axis on which the studies are actually comparable. It also sets the reference against which the nutrient-driven changes below are legible: if copepods are the constant, a decline in their relative share is meaningful in a way that a change in total species count is not.

4Nutrients are restructuring the northern estuaries

The strongest ecological claim available comes from Karati and colleagues, who compared mesozooplankton across six major estuaries on the west coast during late-monsoon and post-monsoon periods. The macro-tidal northern estuaries — Amba and Thane, both in the Mumbai region — carried higher nutrient concentrations than the micro- and meso-tidal systems further south. Elevated nitrate and phosphate in the post-monsoon period indicated domestic and industrial effluent inputs, and these significantly affected community structure.

The mechanism they describe runs from nutrient enrichment to higher phytoplankton standing stock, to depressed dissolved oxygen, to a high-saline eutrophic environment favouring jellyfish. Predation by that jellyfish population on crustacean zooplankton and ichthyoplankton then works against fishery stocks. Their framing is a shift from nutritionally valuable crustacean plankton toward less useful gelatinous forms.

This is the thread most worth pursuing, because the Thane Creek monitoring record independently reports zooplankton density falling while richness stayed roughly constant — the pattern you would predict if the community is being restructured rather than simply depleted. Neither source alone establishes a trend. Read together they justify a targeted resampling of the same Thane stations, which is a fundable proposal rather than a literature exercise.

5The net decides the fauna

The clearest methodological result in this corpus is an absence. The 2022–23 beach survey identified twenty holoplankton genera and reported no meroplankton genera at all. Meanwhile the Karanja estuary, sampled over an overlapping period on the same coastline, found decapod larvae making up 16.75% of total abundance — the second largest contribution after copepods. The mangrove and estuary studies likewise record barnacle nauplii, polychaete larvae, and gastropod and bivalve larvae.

Larval forms did not vanish from Mumbai's water in 2022. The difference is the sampling design: a hand net worked at the surface, in daylight, on exposed beaches, on a roughly fortnightly interval. Each of those choices selects against meroplankton, which concentrates subsurface, pulses with spawning rather than calendar intervals, and is scarce in high-energy surf compared with sheltered estuarine and mangrove water.

The consequence is that the phrase “zooplankton diversity of the Mumbai coast” does not currently refer to a measurable quantity. Four of these studies could each be cited as the diversity of the same coastline and would give incompatible answers, none of them wrong.

6Where the gaps are

  1. No mesh standard. Where mesh size is reported at all it varies, and hand-net work reports none. Without it, abundance figures cannot be converted to a common basis.
  2. Surface and daylight bias. The shore-based studies sample the top of the water column in daytime, missing the vertically migrating fraction that forms much of the nocturnal community.
  3. No monsoon coverage in the shore studies. The beach survey ran September to February; the estuarine work identifies monsoon as the period of peak abundance. The season with the strongest signal is the season least sampled from shore.
  4. Presence without abundance. Several studies, including the openly published beach dataset, record occurrence rather than counts, which precludes Shannon, Simpson or Margalef indices and therefore any comparison with the estuarine literature that reports them.
  5. Almost nothing mobilised to open repositories. The vast majority of this record sits in journal tables. The single Darwin Core archive discussed here is fifty-nine records. A coastline with eighty years of sampling has effectively no machine-readable occurrence data.
  6. Southward and offshore blind spots. Coverage clusters around the port, the creeks and the northern beaches. The 1983 Thal finding that diversity rises offshore has not been followed up in the Mumbai sector.

7A minimum protocol worth agreeing on

The useful output of a review like this is not a species list but a floor for future work. Five requirements would make the next decade of Mumbai student and institute surveys mutually comparable, and none of them requires new funding:

  • Report mesh size, net mouth area and volume filtered, so densities can be expressed per cubic metre.
  • Sample at least one night station per site per season, to capture the migrating fraction.
  • Cover all three seasons including monsoon, even at reduced effort, rather than a continuous five-month block.
  • Record counts, not presence, and report the diversity indices already standard in the regional estuarine literature.
  • Publish the occurrence table as a Darwin Core archive under an open licence at the time of publication, as the beach survey did.

The last point is the one the Mumbai record most obviously lacks, and it is the cheapest to fix.
